Staking pools

The staking pools on the Sequentia network: the stake weight each commands, who lent it, what it has committed on-chain to paying, and how reliably it actually produces. A pool never holds your coins, so what you are choosing here is a block producer, not a custodian.

A pool is a signer that has declared itself one, by committing a payout policy on-chain. That is the only deliberate opt-in there is, and it is what separates an operator asking for your delegation from the many stakers who simply produce blocks for themselves. You may delegate to any signer, and the chain will not stop you, but a signer that has never made a commitment is not running a pool and is not listed here as though it were.

What a pool can and cannot do

It can never spend your coins. Delegating does not move your stake and does not put the pool's key anywhere near it. Your staking output keeps exactly one spending condition, your own key. What you lend sits in a separate little record naming you and the pool, and while that record is unspent the network counts your weight for the pool and expects the pool to sign the blocks.

You can leave whenever you like. Only your key can spend that record, so reclaiming needs nobody's cooperation and nobody's permission. There is no lock, no queue and no notice period on leaving. The weight is yours again the moment the reclaim confirms.

What you are trusting a pool for is the reward, and only the reward. Blocks pay their fees to whoever produced them. A pool that has committed to nothing on-chain keeps all of it, and that is the default rather than an abuse. Read the "Pays out" column before you delegate, and keep reading it afterwards.

A committed policy cannot change behind your back. Any change has to be announced on-chain a fixed number of blocks before it can bind, and every announcement in that window is shown at the top of this page and inside your own wallet. That waiting period is worth exactly as much as the attention you pay to it, which is why leaving is instant: you are meant to have time to read, decide and go.

The two committed modes differ in what they guarantee. A direct policy names an address that every block must pay, so the operator cannot silently redirect the reward, but the chain does not check that the address shares anything with you. A lottery policy makes each block pay one delegator drawn by stake weight from a seed rooted in Bitcoin's proof of work, so the draw cannot be rigged and your share over time is exactly proportional, at the cost of arriving in occasional lumps instead of steadily.

Reliability is the number nobody advertises. A pool holding a tenth of the network's stake should produce about a tenth of the blocks. Well under that means it is offline, missing its slots or losing races, and its delegators are earning nothing for the weight they lent it.

Running one

A staker becomes a pool by committing, on-chain, to how the blocks it produces will pay out. Until it does that it is simply a staker producing for itself, and this page will not list it.

Open the desktop wallet's Staking tab and use Run a staking pool. It offers the usual arrangements to choose from, and spells out what each one means for the people delegating to you before you commit to it:

  • Share everything. Each block pays one of your delegators, drawn by stake weight, and you take nothing beyond what your own stake wins as one participant among them.
  • Share, keeping a commission of 2%, 5% or 10%. Same draw; you keep that share of blocks.
  • Pay one address you commit to, every block. For paying out by an arrangement of your own. The chain stops you redirecting it silently, but does not check that address shares anything with your delegators, and they can see that.

The commitment appears here immediately and binds after the chain's notice period, which is the window in which anyone already delegated to you can read it and leave. Announcing is deliberately the only way onto this page: it is what turns "a staker" into "a pool asking for your delegation", and it is the thing a delegator is actually trusting.

Running headless, with no desktop wallet? The same thing from a node: sequentia-cli announcepayout "lottery" null null null 500 announces a lottery keeping 5%.

Joining a pool

Any Sequentia wallet with a stake can delegate: the desktop wallet, the web wallet, Ambra on Android and Ambra for Chromium. Open its Staking tab, pick a pool, and delegate. The same card shows what that pool has committed to, warns you when it announces a change, and lets you leave in one action.

Leaving needs nobody's cooperation and has no waiting period, so the button is always there. Moving between pools is one action too, and does not touch your stake.

Delegating requires a registered stake. It does not require your wallet to be online afterwards, which is the other reason pools exist: the key that can move your coins never has to touch a block-producing server.

Running headless? sequentia-cli delegatestake "<signer>" to join, listdelegations to see where your stake signs and what is pending against it, and undelegatestake to leave.
